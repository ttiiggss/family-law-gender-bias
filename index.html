Gender Bias in the Australian Family Courts

A compilation of peer-reviewed research, government inquiries, and institute evaluations on parenting arrangements after separation — how the system treats mothers and fathers, and how that has shifted across three decades of reform.

Where family law actually happens

The single most important context: the courts are the exception, not the rule. AIFS’ survey of ~6,000 separated parents found about 3% use courts as their main pathway to making parenting arrangements — predominantly families affected by family violence, child safety concerns, and other complex issues. Most (97%) never go to court, though 16% use family dispute resolution services or lawyers (AIFS, 2022).

What the court-file data shows

  • In both court and non-court ordered arrangements, it is most common for children to spend the majority of their time with their mother and to see their father regularly (AIFS).
  • Court orders are less likely to involve no contact between children and their father than the general separated population: 3% of orders vs 9% overall.
  • Arrangements where children spend most time with their father are more common in litigated orders (10–19%) than in the separated population generally (2%) — litigation is where father-residence outcomes concentrate.
  • Orders for no contact with one parent are rare: 3% of all court orders.

The UNSW / PLOS One language study (2025)

A team of UNSW Sydney data scientists and legal experts used AI to analyse 2,530 custody and parenting judgments across 4,330 documents, handed down between 2001 and 2021, examining how male and female judges spoke to and about male and female parties regarding their capacity to care for children (Akand, Fan, Wobcke, Sisson & San Roque, PLOS One, 2025 — DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0331841).

  • Judgments reflect gender stereotypes: mothers seen mainly as caregivers, fathers judged on their ability to financially provide.
  • The financial-capacity gap is narrowing; the caregiver stereotype is not.
  • Fathers were praised for even limited involvement in childcare, whereas mothers’ efforts were expected.
  • Where the court assessed a father’s capacity to care, he often received the benefit of his new partner’s capacity — satisfaction that he could care for the children “with the assistance of his present wife.”
  • Structural topic modelling found significant gendered differences determined by both the gender of the target and the gender of the judge.

Three decades of reform

  • 1990s — Changes driven partly by successful advocacy from fathers’ rights organisations; policy shifts aimed to support shared care and maintain children’s contact with both parents even where there was a history of family violence or abuse allegations (PLOS One).
  • 2006 — Sweeping reforms: courts required to apply a presumption that equal shared parental responsibility is in the child’s best interests; removal of the terms “contact” and “residence” from the Act; introduction of family dispute resolution services and independent children’s lawyers (APH; AIFS evaluation by Kaspiew et al.).
  • 2012 — Amendments moderated the 2006 changes, repealing a provision that had made it harder for parents (primarily mothers) to resist or restrict contact with a potentially violent or abusive father, and strengthened expectations that courts consider the impacts of family violence on children (PLOS One).
  • 2024 — Most recent reforms shift emphasis away from presumptions about equal parenting, revising the factors courts must consider, including family violence (PLOS One).

Notably, neither the 2006 nor the family-violence amendments produced marked changes in the incidence of shared-time arrangements (Bartlett, Family Court Review).

The contested question: bias against men?

The Parliamentary Joint Select Committee’s inquiry (2020–21) recorded a strong perception, particularly among individual submitters, that the family law system is biased against men and operates in favour of women — a perception some organisations rejected, and others reversed. Better Place Australia cited complaints data showing male family dispute resolution participants consistently felt the system favoured women, attributing this partly to the heavily female FDR practitioner workforce. The committee’s three interim reports (Oct 2020, Mar 2021, and a third on children and the child support formula) made a combined 48 recommendations focused on cost, delays, enforceability of orders, and the family violence framework.

Kaspiew’s earlier doctoral research (2005) found a complex interplay of factors producing different standards of “adequacy” applied to mothers (mostly residence parents) and fathers (mostly contact parents) — and documented that government policy makers had consistently responded to fathers’ rights groups’ claims that the Family Court favours women, with some mothers and children remaining exposed to family violence under court orders.
